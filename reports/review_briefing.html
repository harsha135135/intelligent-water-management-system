Review briefing · Phase III

What we did, what it means, and how to defend it

Everything completed on the campus water-demand forecasting project, written to be read the night before the review — each piece of work paired with what it actually shows, why it changes the project, and the definitions you'll need if someone asks.

00

If you get thirty seconds

We replaced the water-demand forecasting model running on campus with Chronos-2, a pretrained time-series foundation model used zero-shot — no training at all. On a single evaluation grid where all 9 models score the same 188,664 rows, it beats the deployed incumbent at every one of six forecast horizons, and we proved the margin is statistically significant at all six by two independent tests. It also beats a PatchTST trained on this campus by 16.5–18.6% — and so does the incumbent, which says something about the size of the dataset.

It had two known weaknesses. We diagnosed the cause of both and implemented the corrections, measured on data the correction never saw. Its overconfident intervals went from 72% coverage to 78.5% against a target of 80%; its systematic under-forecast went from −10.5% to −1.8%.

What is not done: nothing is deployed and running, and the research paper is not written. Both are designed in detail. Say this before you're asked.

01

The vocabulary you'll be asked about

Six terms carry the whole project. If you can define these cleanly, most follow-up questions answer themselves.

Definition Forecast horizon, and forecast origin

The origin is the moment you stand at; the horizon is how far ahead you predict. A forecast made at 23:00 on Monday for the next 24 hours has a Monday-23:00 origin and a 24-hour horizon. We evaluate six horizons: 6 h, 12 h, 1 d, 2 d, 3 d, 7 d.

Our benchmark uses 24 origins spaced 23 hours apart, not 24. Twenty-four hours would put every origin at the same clock time, so the 6-hour horizon would only ever be scored on the quiet overnight window. Twenty-three is co-prime with 24, so the origins visit every hour of the day exactly once.

Definition Zero-shot

The model was pretrained by Amazon on millions of unrelated time series and is used on our data with no training, no fitting and no fine-tuning. We hand it history, it returns a forecast. This is why the full backtest costs 89 seconds and why there is no per-tank model store to maintain.

Definition MAE and RMSE

MAE (mean absolute error) is the average size of the mistake, in KL/h. RMSE squares the errors before averaging, so it punishes large misses harder. Both are in real units, which makes them readable — but both are dominated by whichever tank moves the most water.

Definition MASE — and why it is not a percentage

MASE is MAE divided by the MAE of a seasonal-naive baseline (the forecast "tomorrow looks like today"). So 1.0 means no better than naive, 0.65 means 35% less error than naive, and above 1.0 means worse than naive.

Never call MASE an accuracy percentage. "MASE 0.65" does not mean 65% accurate. If someone asks for an accuracy figure, give MAE in KL/h, or the 24-hour volume error.

Why it exists: our tanks span three orders of magnitude of demand, and a dead sensor emitting zero forever scores a perfect raw MAE. Ranked by raw MAE, Chronos-2's three "best" tanks are all broken ones — NEW_BLOCK_RO scores 0.0005 by predicting nothing on a tank that does nothing. Dividing by each tank's own naive error removes both problems.

Definition Prediction interval and coverage

Alongside a single number, the model outputs a range — p10 to p90 — and makes a promise: the truth will land inside this range 80% of the time. Coverage is how often that promise actually held.

Ours held 72% of the time, not 80%. The band was too narrow: the model was overconfident. You'd expect to be surprised 1 time in 5 and actually be surprised 1 time in 3.6.

Definition Sensor trust tiers

Before modelling anything we tested each tank against the physical identity Opening + Inflow − Outflow = Closing, judged relative to that tank's own throughput. That splits the campus into 15 healthy, 6 degraded, 3 dead. The three dead tanks are 73–96% zero hours with means under 0.01 KL/h — no signal exists there and a forecast would be theatre.

02

What was done

Nine pieces of work. The first was in place before this round; the rest are new. Each one is stated as: what we did, what it showed, and what it changes.

1 · The benchmark

earlier work
WhatNine forecasting models scored on one shared grid — 24 tanks × 24 origins × 6 horizons — with every model evaluated on identical rows, enforced by an assertion that halts the run if any two models differ.
ShowedChronos-2 zero-shot beats the incumbent NPTS at every horizon on every point metric, by 5.7% to 12.5% MASE. Zero leakage rows, zero duplicates, 6/6 metric tests and 15/15 methodology checks passing.
MeansThis is the result the project rests on. The row-parity guarantee is what makes it a fair comparison rather than a collection of separately-run numbers — the previous work in this repo could not be compared model-to-model, and fixing that was the point.

2 · Statistical significance testing

new
WhatA paired bootstrap over the 24 forecast origins (10,000 resamples) plus a Diebold-Mariano test with the small-sample correction, per horizon and per tank.
ShowedSignificant at all six horizons on both MAE and MASE, by both tests — bootstrap p < 0.0001, DM p between 0.0006 and 0.0069. Per tank at 1 day: 11 significantly better, 9 indistinguishable, 4 significantly worse.
MeansThis closed the single biggest hole. Our own review summary said in writing that no significance test had been run — so the improvement was a point estimate a reviewer could dismiss as noise. It no longer is. And all four significant losses are on degraded or dead sensors: of the 15 healthy tanks, Chronos-2 is better on 8, tied on 7, and worse on none.
Definition Paired bootstrap

We have 24 forecast origins. For each, we compute how much better Chronos-2 was than NPTS. Then we resample those 24 differences with replacement, 10,000 times, and look at the spread of the average. If 95% of those resampled averages still favour Chronos-2, the win is not an accident of which days we happened to pick.

"Paired" means both models are compared on the same rows — which is only possible because of the row-parity guarantee from the benchmark.

3 · Error-pattern analysis

new
WhatBroke the error down by hour of day, by lead time, by demand size, and cumulatively over the window.
ShowedError tracks demand (ρ = 0.958) — it is not failing at particular times. Error grows only +5.6% from day 1 to day 7. All 24 tanks beat the naive baseline. And the bias is negative in all 24 hours of the day.
MeansThree defences in one. The flat lead-time curve means a 7-day forecast is nearly as good as a 1-day forecast — the model learned the repeating daily shape rather than extrapolating a trend, which is what makes weekly planning viable. Positive skill on all 24 tanks kills the "your average is flattered by dead tanks" objection. And a bias present in every hour is systematic, not situational — which is precisely the kind that a correction can fix.

4 · The calibration diagnosis

new most novel
WhatAsked why the intervals under-covered, instead of just reporting that they did. Measured the empirical rate at every quantile, not only the p10–p90 pair.
ShowedThe failure is almost entirely in the lower tail. The p90 is nearly perfect (0.888 against a target of 0.90); the p10 is badly wrong (0.292 against 0.10). Mechanism: 24% of hourly readings are exactly zero, and Chronos-2 places its p10 above zero on 79% of rows — so two thirds of all its lower-tail misses are hours when demand was exactly zero.
MeansThis reframes the one axis where the incumbent wins. NPTS is better calibrated not because it models uncertainty better, but because it is a nonparametric sampler that can reproduce the exact-zero outcome — and a continuous-density foundation model structurally cannot. That is a general finding about foundation models on zero-inflated data, not a quirk of our campus, and it is the most publishable thing in the project.

The detail that shows you did the work properly

The obvious fix — just clamp the lower bound to zero — overshoots, taking coverage to 0.888 against a target of 0.80. We measured that rather than assuming it. It is why the correction we built is asymmetric and fitted, not a clamp.

5 · Calibration, implemented

new
WhatTwo post-hoc corrections, neither requiring retraining. A per-tank volume factor, and conformalised quantile regression per tank with independent lower and upper offsets.
ShowedCoverage 0.741 → 0.785. Volume bias −10.5% → −1.8%. Daily campus error 36.7 → 28.8 KL. And SeasonalNaive-24, which has no native interval at all, went from 0.268 to 0.804.
MeansTwo items our own future-work list flagged as the highest-value next steps are now done and measured — on a window the correction never saw. This is the difference between "we know what's wrong" and "we fixed it".
Definition Conformal calibration

Take 60 days of forecasts the model already made. Measure how far the truth actually fell outside the stated band. Take the 90th percentile of those misses in each direction, and widen the band by exactly that much. Per tank.

That is the entire method — "how wrong has this band been historically? add that much slack." No retraining, no new model, and it comes with a mathematical guarantee that the widened band hits its target rate on exchangeable data.

Ours is asymmetric: because the diagnosis showed the failure is lower-tail, we push the bottom down much harder than the top, and clip the lower bound at zero — which is what finally lets the interval say "it might be nothing at all".

Definition Volume bias correction

A different problem from the interval. Not about confidence — about the level being consistently wrong in one direction. Chronos-2's forecasts summed to 10.5% below actual.

Fix: for each tank, divide total actual by total predicted over the calibration window to get one multiplier, then scale future forecasts by it. Seventeen tanks got a normal factor between 0.90 and 1.63; four hit a safety clip at 2.0 (one had a raw ratio of 20× — a broken sensor, not a model error, which is what the clip is for); three were too dead to correct.

The methodological point they may probe — have this ready

Calibration parameters are fitted on 8 Jan – 8 Mar and reported on 9 Mar – 22 Apr. Strictly later, strictly disjoint, and the code raises an error if the two windows touch.

Why it matters: if you fit the correction on the same days you then measure coverage on, the coverage comes out right by construction — you tuned it to fit, and the number means nothing. Keeping them separate is what makes 0.785 an honest number rather than a circular one.

6 · The continuous 45-day holdout

new
What45 consecutive daily forecasts — each origin at 23:00 predicting the next 24 hours — so the forecasts tile the window once, with no gaps and no overlap, and can be drawn as one continuous line. Three figures, four representative tanks each.
ShowedChronos-2 reproduces 89% of the real day-to-day variation and correlates +0.65 with it. NPTS reproduces 6% and correlates −0.31 — it is effectively a flat line at the long-run average, on every tank.
MeansThis is the strongest operational argument, and it is not visible in MASE at all. For answering "how much water does the campus need tomorrow", the incumbent carries almost no information beyond the average. Chronos-2 carries most of it. Show the two charts side by side — the picture makes the case faster than any table.

Why the seasonal-naive model is in the comparison at all

It comes last on every table, so someone will ask. It is not a competitor — it is the measuring instrument. MASE is defined as the ratio to it, so seasonal naive must score ≈ 1.0, and it does (0.967–1.069). That is the checksum on the entire metric: if it failed, every MASE number in the study would be wrong, and there is a unit test asserting it.

It also earns its place in the 45-day panels, where three models give three distinct failure modes: Chronos-2 tracks but sits low (a level problem, fixable), NPTS doesn't track at all (an information problem, not fixable post-hoc), and seasonal naive tracks perfectly but one day late (a timing problem). Without the third you cannot tell whether tracking this data is even possible.

7 · Real-time system design

new design only
WhatSix documents specifying the production system: architecture with 10 diagrams, an 11-phase implementation plan, API design, data model, demo plan, and a safety document for automated motor control.
ShowedA design where the data source is a swappable adapter, so replaying historical data and reading live data use the same pipeline. Motor safety is separated into layers: the forecast can never command a pump directly — a separate safety gate can only veto or clamp.
MeansThis is what turns "we have a good model" into "we have a plan to deploy it". It is design, not implementation — say so plainly. Its value at the review is that the deployment gap has a concrete, costed answer rather than a shrug.

8 · The repository

new
WhatPublic repo with the full pipeline, dataset, all 26 figures, metrics, tests and documentation — 43 MB, with model binaries excluded because they regenerate in about an hour.
MeansEverything claimed is reproducible from source by anyone. Three commands regenerate every number in the study.

9 · The deep-learning control — PatchTST

new
WhatTrained PatchTST on this campus and scored it on the identical grid — same 24 origins, same six horizons, same 188,664 rows, same row-parity assertion. Two configurations: AutoGluon's defaults, and a second at the settings the PatchTST paper uses for hourly data (context 512 h, 100 epochs), because Chronos-2 sees 2,048 hours of context and a win over a handicapped opponent is not a win.
ShowedChronos-2 zero-shot is 16.5–18.6% better in MASE than the stronger PatchTST at every horizon, significant at all six by both tests, winning 120/144 tank×horizon cells. Per tank at 1 day: 19 significantly better, 4 indistinguishable, 1 significantly worse. PatchTST needed 55 min of training against Chronos-2's 89 s of inference.
MeansThis closes the last obvious hole: every previous opponent was statistical, so "you never tried a real deep model" was a fair objection. It no longer is. The more interesting result is that the trained transformer also loses to the incumbent — on 24 short, noisy, zero-inflated series, training a transformer from scratch buys less than a well-chosen non-parametric method, and far less than a pretrained one. That is the argument for a foundation model, stated as a measurement rather than a preference.

How this changes the project

Five things are true now that were not true before this round of work.

The sentence that captures the whole contribution

We did not just show that a foundation model beats the incumbent. We showed by how much, proved it wasn't luck, identified exactly which tanks it can and cannot be trusted on, diagnosed the structural reason its uncertainty estimates were wrong, and fixed them.

04

Likely questions, with answers

The nine most probable, in roughly the order they tend to come.

Your model isn't trained on your data at all. How can it work?

It was pretrained on millions of time series and has learned what demand-like signals look like in general. Our data has strong daily structure it can recognise without ever having seen it. The evidence is that it beats a model fitted specifically on our data at every horizon — and it costs 89 seconds instead of 26 minutes.

MASE 0.65 — so it's 65% accurate?

No — MASE is a ratio, not a percentage. 1.0 means no better than a naive baseline, so 0.65 means 35% less error than that baseline. If you want an accuracy figure in real units, the 24-hour campus volume is predicted to within about 11%.

Isn't the average flattered by the dead tanks?

That's exactly why we don't use raw MAE — ranked by MAE our "best" tanks are the broken ones. MASE removes that. And we measured skill against the naive baseline per tank: all 24 are positive, and skill does not decline with tank size.

How do you know it isn't just luck on the days you picked?

Paired bootstrap over the 24 origins, 10,000 resamples, plus a Diebold-Mariano test. Significant at all six horizons on both metrics, all p < 0.01. At the 7-day horizon it wins on 24 origins out of 24.

Your intervals are miscalibrated.

They were — 72% against 80%. We found the reason: a quarter of hours are exactly zero, and a continuous model puts its lower bound above zero, so every quiet hour falls out the bottom. Two thirds of the misses are exactly-zero hours.

We then fixed it with asymmetric conformal calibration — 78.5%, measured on a window the calibration never saw.

Why does calibration make the hourly MAE slightly worse?

Because MAE is minimised by predicting the median, and the bias correction scales toward the mean. On a right-skewed series those differ. Over 24 hours the bias compounds while the noise cancels, so daily volume error improves 21% even though hourly MAE rises 4%. The correction is for volume and refill sizing, which is what it's for.

Did you try an actual deep-learning model, or only statistical baselines?

Both. PatchTST — the same patched-transformer family Chronos-2 belongs to — trained on this campus at two configurations, on the identical rows. Chronos-2 beats the stronger one by 16.5–18.6% MASE at every horizon, significant at all six.

The part worth saying out loud: PatchTST also loses to the incumbent. Twenty-four short, zero-inflated series is not enough data to train a transformer from scratch — which is exactly the situation a pretrained model exists for.

Is this running anywhere?

No. There's a browser extension that displays real forecasts from a precomputed file, but nothing is serving live. The production architecture is fully specified — data model, APIs, safety layers, an 11-phase plan — and phases 1 to 3 are the next concrete step.

What would you do next?

Three things, in order. Write the paper — the zero-inflation calibration finding is a genuine contribution and the material already exists. Build a minimal live slice to close the deployment gap. And attack GJBC_LAW_BLOCK_3__A1, the one healthy sensor the model genuinely fails on — it needs a spike model, not a smoother.

05

What isn't done

Volunteer these. Being first to name your own gaps reads as rigour; being caught on them reads as a hole in the work.

Nothing is deployed

The Chrome dock runs offline from a bundled file. The container stack that exists points at models we retired. Do not describe the dock as a deployment — describing it accurately is more credible than describing it generously.

The research paper isn't written

All the material exists — methodology, results, per-tank analysis, limitations. It is assembly work, not new research.

System testing is partial

6/6 metric tests and 15/15 methodology checks pass, which validates the measurement apparatus. There's no integration test suite, because the system it would test isn't built. Say that rather than presenting model tests as system tests.

PatchTST was not architecture-searched

Two configurations were run — AutoGluon's defaults and the PatchTST paper's hourly settings — not a per-tank sweep. The claim is that a competently configured PatchTST loses by 16.5–18.6% on 24 series this short, not that no PatchTST could ever win, and not a statement about deep forecasting given substantially more data.

Two honest limitations in the results themselves

The evaluation covers one 23-day window in one season — the improvement figures shouldn't be extrapolated to a monsoon. And the daily-total confidence bands transfer worse than the hourly ones (63% at campus level against 80% nominal), because tank errors are correlated and don't cancel in the sum. Per-tank they're fine, at a median of 0.781.

One loose end we created

The architecture gates a per-tank model router on a significance test across at least 4 of 6 horizons. We ran that test at the 1-day horizon only, so the gate can't yet be satisfied either way. It's a small extension — worth mentioning as known and scoped rather than leaving it to be found.
